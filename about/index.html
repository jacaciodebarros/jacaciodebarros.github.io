I am a Professor of Philosophy at San Francisco State University, where I have taught since 2007 and where I direct the School of Liberal Studies. From 1995 to 2011, I also held an Associate Professorship in the Physics Department at the Federal University of Juiz de Fora, in Minas Gerais, Brazil. I am a Corresponding Member of the Brazilian Academy of Philosophy.

I was trained as a physicist: my bachelor’s degree is from the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, and my master’s degree and doctorate are both from the Brazilian Center for Research in Physics (CBPF) in Rio de Janeiro. My work has moved steadily since then from physics toward philosophy.

An interdisciplinary background

My work has been interdisciplinary for most of my career. I started in mathematical physics and quantum cosmology: my doctoral dissertation was on undecidability and incompleteness in physics, and my master’s thesis on Cohen’s generic sets and their applications to physics. From there I moved to the foundations and philosophy of quantum mechanics, to probability and contextuality, and to questions about cognition and mind.

Since 2007 my academic home at SF State has also been an interdisciplinary one: I teach the theory and practice of interdisciplinary inquiry as well as the physical sciences and mathematics, and I have directed the School of Humanities and Liberal Studies and, since 2022, the School of Liberal Studies. My courses are described in the Teaching section, and my research in the Research section.

Research interests

Most of my research is on the foundations and philosophy of physics, particularly the foundations of quantum mechanics, and how the mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics informs us about problems outside of physics. Most recently I have been focusing on indistinguishability in physics and how it relates to the principles that define quantum mechanics. I have also worked for many years on cognition, mind, and the brain.

  • Foundations of quantum mechanics
  • Probability and contextuality
  • Mind and brain
  • Quantum cosmology and gravity (earlier work)

A statement of purpose

From the mission statement written for the previous version of this site.

As a professor, particularly in the School of Liberal Studies at SF State, I enjoy tremendous academic freedom. It is, therefore, my responsibility to work on issues that are not only fun but also relevant. I will strive to choose research and teaching topics I enjoy knowing about and working on. I will keep on working to achieve a deeper understanding and never believe that whatever knowledge I have is sufficient. I believe that relevance comes from depth.

I also believe I owe my students much more than just depth of knowledge. As an educator, I will connect my intellectual interests to those in my classes by making my teaching subjects more attractive, listening to my students, and incorporating their needs. As an educator, I know how science and mathematics can become sources of inequality. I will work to reduce those inequalities, fostering classroom environments where all my students can learn.

Academic lineage

Contrary to ordinary genealogy, which grows exponentially when you move back in time, one’s academic genealogy only includes their Ph.D. advisor, the advisor’s advisor, and so on. So I’ll start with my main advisor, Francisco Antonio Doria. Doria got his Ph.D. from the Brazilian Center for Research in Physics (CBPF) in 1977, and his advisor was Leopoldo Nachbin. The usual mathematical-genealogy chain then runs from Nachbin to Laurent Schwartz, and from Schwartz to Georges Valiron. Valiron got his doctorate from the University of Paris in 1914 under Émile Borel, and Borel’s advisor was Gaston Darboux.

Genealogical databases take this line much further back, but that far back the idea of a formal Ph.D. advisor does not really apply.

Patrick Suppes was another major mentor and collaborator. He earned his Ph.D. in Philosophy at Columbia University in 1950 under Ernest Nagel. Nagel was strongly influenced by Morris R. Cohen, who earned his Harvard doctorate in 1906 and studied with William James, Hugo Münsterberg, and Josiah Royce.

As a curiosity, my Erdős number is 4. I published with Pat Suppes, who published with Dana Scott. Dana Scott published with Alfred Tarski, who finally published with Paul Erdős.
